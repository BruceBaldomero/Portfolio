Live service status board with Teams alerts

One page that watches our suppliers and our own websites every minute, catches certificate faults Windows hides, and tells the right people in Microsoft Teams without the noise.

The problem

Every day, someone on the support team opened each supplier’s status page and our internal monitoring by hand, checked them one by one, then ticked a checklist in Microsoft Teams.

It was slow and easy to forget. Worse, nobody heard about a problem that started between checks.

What I built

One web page that checks 7 third-party suppliers (payments, email, helpdesk, direct debit and banking providers) and 21 of our own websites every 60 seconds. Each one shows green, amber, red or grey, and the board posts to Microsoft Teams when something changes.

How it works

Suppliers

Every minute, the board reads each supplier’s official status feed: the machine-readable data behind their public status page. It understands the Atlassian Statuspage, status.io, StatusHub and Freshstatus formats.

  • Operational shows green.
  • Degraded or maintenance shows amber.
  • Outage shows red.

Each card quotes the supplier’s own explanation and links to their page. If a feed can’t be read or changes shape, the card shows grey (“can’t check”). It never shows a false green.

Our websites

Every minute, the board connects to each site the way a customer’s browser does. It checks that the HTTPS certificate is valid, issued for the right name, not expiring within 30 days, and sent with its complete chain. Then it reads the response code.

Why the certificate check matters

After a certificate change at work, a site served its certificate without the “intermediate” certificate. Windows quietly fills in the missing piece, so it looked fine on our machines. Other devices and apps refused to connect.

The board checks strictly. It uses its own trusted certificate list and never fills gaps, so it sees what the strictest customer device sees. On its first run it flagged another site with the same fault.

Teams alerts for two audiences

  • Management’s chat gets outages only, the “it’s fixed” message, and one summary at 9am every day.
  • The support team’s chat gets every change, plus summaries at 9, 11, 1, 3 and 5.

Every message links back to the board and to a certificate report.

Certificate report

A second page lists every site’s certificate issuer, expiry date and days left, with problems first and a CSV download.

Built to be light

  • Zero dependencies. Plain Node.js using only built-in modules. No npm install, no build step, no database.
  • Tiny footprint. About 55 MB of memory and almost no CPU.
  • Built, not bought. I trialled an off-the-shelf tool (Uptime Kuma) first. I built my own because I needed to read each supplier’s own explanation and route alerts to different audiences.

Making alerts useful, not noisy

Alert noise was the hardest problem. Alerts that cry wolf get muted, so every rule below exists to keep messages rare and trustworthy.

  • Problems now, recoveries when stable

    Problems alert immediately. Recoveries wait until things have been stable for 15 minutes. One supplier incident that flapped back and forth would have sent about 25 messages. It now sends 2.

  • Confirm before alerting

    A site must still be down a minute later before anyone is alerted, so a one-off network blip never reaches Teams. Certificate faults alert at once.

  • Never repeat itself

    It remembers what it has already announced, so restarts never repeat messages. If two copies run at once, only one posts, using a lock file with a heartbeat.

  • No false alarms when it’s offline

    If the board loses its own internet connection, everything goes grey rather than red.

  • UK time, all year

    Schedules run on UK time and stay correct through daylight-saving changes.

Tested properly

40 automated checks, including a replica of the real certificate fault, 12 back-to-back restarts, two copies running at once, and a simulated clock change.

Safe by design

Read-only, using public information only. No logins or passwords stored. Webhook secrets are kept out of source control, and test endpoints are disabled in production.

Outcome

  • Replaced the manual daily checklist with live monitoring and automatic alerts.
  • Adopted by the business and hosted internally.

A really good addition to visibility.

The CIO, who asked for it on the office wall and for a show-and-tell

What I learned

Designing alerts for different audiences matters as much as detecting problems. Noisy alerts get ignored, however accurate they are.

And “it works on my machine” can hide real faults. The certificate issue looked fine on our machines, and was still broken for other devices and apps.
